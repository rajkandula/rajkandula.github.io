Many of the most damaging trajectories in human life share a common root: no one was paying sustained, caring attention at the moments when small interventions would have mattered most. LLM agents make sustained, personalized attention technically feasible for the first time — but the obvious architecture, pervasive institutional monitoring, is both ethically unacceptable and counterproductive, because coerced observation destroys the trust that mentorship, disclosure, and behavior change depend on.

This paper proposes a consent-first alternative: every individual owns a **Personal Mentor Agent (PMA)** supporting their learning, health habits, and long-term goals, while institutions operate **Institutional Coordinator Agents (ICAs)** that can interact with a person's PMA only through explicit, revocable, scoped consent.

**Contributions**

* A design analysis showing why consent-first architectures dominate surveillance architectures on effectiveness grounds, not only ethical ones.
* A reference architecture including an agent-to-agent consent protocol, a local-first personal data vault, and human-in-the-loop escalation for safety-critical events.
* Illustrative scenarios across education, healthcare, homes, and workplaces.
* A concrete evaluation plan with falsifiable hypotheses and measurable outcomes.

The central claim: consent is not a constraint on such systems, but the mechanism that makes them work.
